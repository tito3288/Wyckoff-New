**A Wyckoff Consulting Thought Paper**

If you are thinking about selling your company in the next 18 to 24 months, the work that influences valuation has already begun.

Most owners naturally focus on EBITDA, financial statements, contracts, operations, legal structure, and management depth. They should.

But sophisticated buyers are also asking another set of questions: Where does future revenue come from? How dependent is growth on the owner? How strong is the company’s position in the market? How repeatable is customer acquisition? What happens when the current owner walks out the door?

Those are marketing questions—and they can become valuation questions.

A buyer is not simply purchasing what your company earned yesterday. They are underwriting their confidence in what the business can produce tomorrow.

That is why the 18–24 months before a transaction should not be the time to “do more marketing.” It should be the time to make the company’s growth story more credible, measurable, transferable, and scalable.

## 1. Reduce the Owner’s Role in Revenue Generation

In many privately held companies, the founder is still one of the most important sources of new business. The owner has the relationships, reputation, industry knowledge, and personal network that helped build the company.

That is an asset while you own the business. It can become a risk when you want to sell it.

A buyer has to ask: “What happens to revenue when this person leaves?”

If the answer is unclear, perceived risk rises. And risk affects value.

The objective before a sale is to transfer the company’s commercial strength from the owner into the organization itself.

- Document the sales process and key customer relationships.

- Build market visibility around the company—not only the founder.

- Create repeatable lead-generation channels.

- Strengthen sales tools, messaging, and CRM discipline.

- Develop other leaders who can credibly represent the business.

**You want the buyer to see a company that can continue growing without needing the previous owner to create every opportunity.**

## 2. Build a Predictable Revenue Engine

Buyers love growth. They love predictable growth even more.

A company that cannot explain where future opportunities come from forces a buyer to make assumptions. A company with a measurable commercial engine gives the buyer evidence.

The question is not simply, “Did revenue grow?” It is, “Can you demonstrate why it grew—and can that growth be repeated?”

- Where leads originate.

- Cost to acquire opportunities and customers.

- Pipeline by source, segment, and stage.

- Conversion rates.

- Sales cycle length.

- Customer retention and expansion.

- Marketing contribution to revenue.

**Eighteen months before a sale is enough time to establish baselines, improve the system, and show a meaningful trend. Starting three months before diligence is too late.**

## 3. Fix Customer Concentration Before the Buyer Prices It In

A $20 million company with one customer representing 35% of revenue is not the same asset as a $20 million company with a diversified customer base.

Customer concentration may be an operational reality, but marketing can help leadership attack it deliberately before a transaction.

The goal is not to chase random revenue simply to dilute a percentage. The goal is to identify attractive adjacent customers, segments, verticals, or geographies where the company has a credible right to win.

- Define the ideal client profile beyond the largest accounts.

- Identify whitespace in existing and adjacent markets.

- Build targeted account and demand-generation strategies.

- Strengthen customer retention and cross-sell programs.

- Create evidence that new revenue sources are developing.

**Every percentage point of dependency you reduce can strengthen the story that the business is durable beyond a handful of relationships.**

## 4. Make the Company Look as Valuable as It Actually Is

I have seen excellent companies that look average from the outside.

They have sophisticated capabilities, outstanding customers, decades of expertise, strong margins, and remarkable people—but their website, messaging, digital presence, sales materials, and market positioning tell none of that story.

That matters because a potential buyer will experience your brand long before management presentations and diligence rooms.

The company’s market presence should support the valuation story—not undermine it.

- Does our positioning reflect the company we have become?

- Is our differentiation obvious?

- Do our website and sales materials communicate our capabilities and scale?

- Can we demonstrate customer success with credible proof?

- Does our digital footprint reinforce leadership in the markets we serve?

**This is not cosmetic work. Strong positioning can help a buyer understand why customers choose you, why competitors cannot easily replace you, and why the business has room to grow.**

## 5. Build the Growth Story the Buyer Can Believe

One of the most important things management will eventually present is the company’s future.

The buyer already has historical financials. What they need to believe is the next chapter.

Where can this company grow? Which markets remain underpenetrated? Which services can expand? What customer segments represent opportunity? What does the pipeline indicate? What commercial investments could accelerate results?

The strongest growth story is not a slide full of optimistic projections. It is a strategy supported by evidence.

- Clear market opportunity.

- Defined ideal customers.

- Documented competitive advantages.

- A credible pipeline.

- Repeatable sales and marketing processes.

- Measurable KPIs.

- A prioritized roadmap for future growth.

**The objective is to make the buyer think: “We can see exactly how to grow this business from here.”**

## The Marketing Mistake Owners Make Before a Sale

The mistake is waiting until the company is officially “for sale” to start improving the marketing story.

At that point, you can redesign a website. You can create a presentation. You can clean up messaging.

What you cannot manufacture overnight is 18 months of evidence.

You cannot instantly create a diversified pipeline, demonstrate consistent lead generation, reduce founder dependency, establish market authority, or prove that a new growth strategy works.

Time is one of the most valuable assets an owner has before a transaction.

Use it.

## What a Fractional CMO Brings to the Table

The role of a Fractional Chief Marketing Officer during the pre-sale window is different from traditional marketing leadership.

The objective is not vanity metrics. It is not more followers, more impressions, or simply more activity.

The objective is to strengthen the commercial characteristics of the business that support growth, reduce perceived risk, and contribute to enterprise value.

Wyckoff Consulting can work alongside the CEO, CFO, sales leadership, ownership, board, and transaction advisors to evaluate the company through a buyer’s commercial lens.

- Where is revenue too dependent on the owner?

- How predictable is the pipeline?

- Where does customer concentration create risk?

- Does the market positioning support the company’s true value?

- Are sales and marketing aligned around measurable growth?

- Can leadership clearly articulate the next three years of opportunity?

- What can realistically be improved before the company goes to market?

**Then we build and execute the marketing roadmap around the areas that matter most.**

## Five Questions Every CEO Should Ask 18–24 Months Before a Sale

**1. If I disappeared tomorrow, how much of our revenue engine would disappear with me?**

**2. Can we show a buyer exactly where new business comes from and how repeatable it is?**

**3. Are we overly dependent on any customer, relationship, salesperson, or market?**

**4. Does our brand and market position reflect the quality and value of the company we are selling?**

**5. Can we present a credible, evidence-based growth story for the next owner?**

## You Are Not Just Selling EBITDA

You are selling confidence.

Confidence that customers will stay. Confidence that new customers can be acquired. Confidence that the company is differentiated. Confidence that revenue does not walk out the door with the founder. Confidence that there is another chapter of growth still available.

The financial statements tell the buyer what the business has done.

Your marketing and growth infrastructure help demonstrate what the business can become.

That distinction matters.

Wyckoff Consulting works with established $3 million to $50 million companies at critical business inflection points—including owners preparing for an eventual exit.

Eighteen to twenty-four months before a sale is not too early to begin preparing the marketing side of the business.

It may be the most valuable time you have.

Because when the buyer finally arrives, you do not want to be explaining what you plan to build.

You want to show them what is already working.

**Build the growth story before you sell the company.**
