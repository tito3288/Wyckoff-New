**A Wyckoff Consulting Thought Paper**

Your Chief Marketing Officer or senior marketing executive just resigned.

Or perhaps you made the difficult decision that they were no longer the right person to lead the organization forward.

Either way, there’s suddenly an empty chair at the executive table.

The natural response is:

**“We need to start looking for a replacement.”**

You probably do.

But that’s not your most immediate problem.

Your immediate problem is determining **who is going to lead marketing tomorrow morning.**

For companies generating $3 million to $50 million in revenue, losing a senior marketing executive can create a leadership vacuum that reaches far beyond the marketing department.

Marketing doesn’t stop because the executive left.

Your sales team still needs leads.

Campaigns are still running.

Agencies are still spending money.

Customers are still seeing your brand.

Competitors are still pursuing your prospects.

Budgets are still being spent.

And your growth expectations haven’t changed.

The question isn’t whether the company can survive several months without a senior marketing executive.

Of course it can.

The better question is:

**What could you lose while that seat remains empty?**

Here are five risks every CEO and executive team should consider.

## 1. Marketing Activity Continues. Marketing Leadership Doesn’t.

This is perhaps the greatest misconception following the departure of a marketing executive.

Everything still appears to be working.

Emails go out.

Social media gets posted.

Agencies continue their campaigns.

Trade shows remain scheduled.

The website stays online.

Marketing meetings continue.

From the outside, very little appears to have changed.

But **activity and leadership are not the same thing.**

Someone still needs to decide:

What should we prioritize?

What should we stop?

Where should we invest?

What’s working?

What’s wasting money?

What does sales need from marketing?

Where is our next growth opportunity?

Without executive ownership, marketing can quickly become a collection of activities rather than a coordinated business strategy.

**The lights may still be on. That doesn’t mean someone is steering the ship.**

## 2. The CEO Becomes the CMO by Default

When senior marketing leadership disappears, responsibility usually moves upward.

Suddenly the CEO, President, COO, or VP of Sales starts approving campaigns, reviewing agencies, evaluating marketing ideas, attending marketing meetings, and answering questions previously handled by the marketing executive.

There are two problems with this.

First, these executives already have full-time jobs.

Second, being an accomplished CEO or sales leader doesn’t automatically make someone an experienced marketing executive.

The result is predictable.

Decisions slow down.

Marketing becomes reactive.

Strategic initiatives get postponed.

And highly compensated executives spend valuable time managing functions outside their primary responsibilities.

The company doesn’t eliminate the CMO role when the CMO leaves.

**Someone else simply starts doing the job.**

## 3. Your Best Marketing People May Be the Next to Leave

Senior executives sometimes underestimate how destabilizing leadership changes can be for the people underneath them.

Your marketing team is asking questions immediately:

Who am I reporting to?

Is my job safe?

Will the new leader change everything?

Are our current initiatives still priorities?

Does leadership still believe in what we’re doing?

Should I start looking elsewhere?

Your strongest employees usually have the most options.

If uncertainty continues for months, you risk turning the loss of one executive into the loss of an entire team.

That’s why the first responsibility following a leadership departure isn’t simply replacing the executive.

**It’s restoring confidence.**

People need leadership.

They need priorities.

They need decisions.

And they need to know someone is accountable.

## 4. Sales and Marketing Can Drift Apart—Fast

In growing companies, one of marketing’s most important responsibilities is supporting revenue.

When the senior marketing leader disappears, the relationship between sales and marketing can deteriorate quickly.

Sales starts creating its own materials.

Messaging becomes inconsistent.

Lead quality isn’t discussed.

Marketing pursues one priority while sales pursues another.

Customer feedback stops reaching the people shaping marketing strategy.

Eventually, two departments that should operate as one revenue engine begin operating independently.

And the CEO often doesn’t realize it until pipeline performance starts suffering.

**A marketing leadership gap can quickly become a revenue alignment gap.**

## 5. The Rush to Replace the Executive Can Create an Even Bigger Problem

This is where urgency can become dangerous.

The company feels exposed.

The team wants leadership.

The CEO wants the problem solved.

So the executive search becomes:

**“How quickly can we get someone in the chair?”**

That’s the wrong question.

A leadership transition creates something valuable that companies rarely get:

**A moment to reconsider what the company actually needs.**

Before replacing the previous executive, ask:

What worked under the previous structure?

What didn’t?

What does the business need during its next three years—not its previous three?

Do we need a strategist?

A brand builder?

A demand-generation executive?

A team builder?

A growth executive?

A commercially minded CMO who can align marketing with sales and finance?

The person who was right for the company at $10 million may not be the person needed to reach $25 million.

**Don’t automatically replace the person. Reevaluate the position.**

## The Hidden Cost of the Empty Chair

Executive searches take time.

Finding the right person should take time.

But the business can’t wait for the search process to finish before marketing leadership resumes.

Imagine six months without clear executive marketing leadership.

That’s six months of:

Delayed decisions.

Unchallenged agency spending.

Missed market opportunities.

Disconnected sales and marketing teams.

Uncertain employees.

Unclear accountability.

And potentially millions of dollars in revenue opportunities passing through the business.

The salary savings from an empty executive position can become insignificant compared with the cost of lost momentum.

**An empty chair can be surprisingly expensive.**

## Three Questions Every CEO Should Ask Immediately

### 1. Who owns our marketing strategy tomorrow morning?

Not who manages the tasks.

**Who owns the decisions?**

### 2. What initiatives or revenue opportunities are at risk while this position remains open?

Identify them now—not six months from now.

### 3. Are we replacing the executive we had—or hiring the executive our next stage of growth requires?

Those may be two very different people.

## You Don’t Need to Rush the Hire. You Do Need to Fill the Leadership Gap.

There is an important distinction.

You should take the necessary time to find the right permanent executive.

But you don’t need to operate without executive marketing leadership while you search.

An experienced **Fractional Chief Marketing Officer** can immediately step into the leadership gap, stabilize the marketing organization, evaluate current strategy and spending, align marketing with sales, manage agencies and internal resources, and help leadership determine what the permanent role should ultimately look like.

For some organizations, that means serving as an interim CMO until the permanent executive arrives.

For others, the process reveals that the organization doesn’t need a full-time CMO at all.

Either way, the objective is the same:

**Protect momentum while making the right long-term decision.**

Because when your senior marketing executive leaves, the biggest risk isn’t the empty position on the organizational chart.

**It’s allowing your growth strategy to leave with them.**
